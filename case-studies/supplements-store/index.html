Case study · Supplements e-commerce

Supplements store: £9,826 and £18,401 across different reporting periods

Published on 2026-07-08 · EmailFlow

How to read these results

This case study reports an individual store’s results. Email-attributed revenue depends on the platform and attribution window; it is not a controlled measure of incremental sales. Client identity is withheld, and this page does not provide an independent verification of the account data. Results are not a forecast for another store. Source reports, attribution windows and click/open credit settings are not supplied on this page. Customer market and changes in traffic, promotions and seasonality are not fully documented, so this comparison cannot isolate their effects.

The situation before: "it works" doesn't mean "it's maxed out"

The February report looks perfectly respectable — £9,826 from email. But under the surface were the typical gaps of a DIY setup: flows with gaps in their content and conditions, no exit conditions or segmentation, and campaigns living on a "whenever there's time" rhythm — 1–2 a month, with no calendar and no testing. In the supplements niche that's especially costly: the product is consumable, replenishment timing depends on the product, usage and actual repeat purchase data.

What we did

  1. Rebuilt the automations through our ecommerce email marketing agency optimization process on Omnisend: expanded the existing flows, added missing ones — and, crucially for supplements, replenishment logic in the win-back sequence, synced to the product's consumption cycle;
  2. Introduced campaign regularity: from 1–2 a month to a campaign every week — ingredient education, protocols, offers (why this works — a separate article);
  3. Segmentation: buyers grouped by product category and purchase cycle — a supplements customer gets an email about their product, not everything at once;
  4. Took over all production — copy, design, sending, reporting. Client product information and approvals remained necessary.

The most interesting detail of this case: the larger difference in reported totals was in campaigns (£3,993 → £9,667). The automations already existed and were doing some work — but the reports do not isolate the impact of changing campaign frequency. That's the mirror image of the fashion store case — and a reminder that there's no one-size-fits-all recipe: an audit first shows you which side of the channel is hungry.

What this case teaches

Review existing performance before expanding the programme. The difference between the reported totals is £8,575, but it is not established additional revenue per month or evidence of unused capacity.

Buying cycle matters. Replenishment messages should reflect the products and customer purchase history, rather than assume a universal repeat interval.

Unequal reporting windows: February 2026 has 28 days. May 23–June 23 spans 31 elapsed days, or 32 calendar dates if both endpoints are included; the report’s inclusion convention is unconfirmed. The raw revenue ratio is 1.87x (+87.3%), without normalisation. Campaign and automation components total £18,400, £1 below the reported £18,401; source precision needs confirmation. Store revenue also rose from £26,340 to £40,149. Attribution, traffic, promotions and seasonality can affect this comparison; the totals do not isolate the effect of management.
