How to measure email revenue without trusting attribution blindly

Published on 2026-11-04 · EmailFlow

Your email dashboard says £12,000. Your store report gives email a different number. Your advertising platform claims some of the same orders.

Which number should you believe?

Before choosing a winner, ask what each report is counting. An order can involve several messages, a search, an advert and a direct visit. Different systems can assign credit differently without creating an extra sale in your bank account.

At EmailFlow, we use Omnisend to understand recorded customer activity and review the overall statistics. It is a useful starting point for deciding what to improve. But a platform report needs context: what happened in the store, what the promotion cost and which reporting rules were applied.

This guide takes you through a practical monthly review, using a hypothetical Shopify store. No figures below are EmailFlow client results.

Start with three different questions

Email measurement becomes easier when you separate three questions that often get bundled into one revenue figure.

What did the store sell? Review orders and sales in Shopify, checking the chosen sales definition, dates and adjustments. This gives you the commercial context for the month.

Which orders received email credit? Omnisend's attribution report answers this according to its rules and your settings. It helps you compare messages and investigate customer journeys.

How many additional purchases happened because of email? This is a question about incremental impact. Answering it requires a credible comparison with what would have happened without the message or programme.

The third answer does not automatically equal the second. A returning customer might have bought anyway. Equally, an email could help someone decide to buy even when its contribution is not captured in the report.

Use attribution to guide investigation. Use commercial outcomes and well-designed comparisons to judge growth.

Write down the rules before comparing the numbers

Imagine a store called Example Goods reviewing October. The owner exports a campaign report, opens Shopify and immediately sees a mismatch.

Before rewriting the strategy, create a short measurement note:

SettingWhat Example Goods should record
PeriodStart date, end date and time zone
CurrencyThe currency displayed in each report
Sales definitionGross, net or total sales, and included charges
AttributionEligible engagement, window and channel rules
Reporting dateMessage date or order date
AdjustmentsHow returns, refunds and cancellations appear
ScopeCampaigns, automations, email and other channels

This is not administrative busywork. It prevents a change in reporting from being presented as a change in business performance.

Keep the note with each export. If you make a settings change, document the date and reason, then check whether past results are restated. Do not compare an old screenshot with a newly calculated dashboard as though both used the same method.

Understand what Omnisend is crediting

Omnisend's published guidance describes engagement-based attribution, configurable windows and a last-touch rule between messages. It also warns that changing attribution settings recalculates historical reporting. Export a baseline before making changes. Omnisend: how sales attribution works

For your review, inspect the actual account settings rather than assuming a default. Ask which tracked interactions qualify, how long an order remains eligible and whether privacy-related opens are included.

Then inspect several real attributed orders. What message received credit? Was the customer new or returning? Did the purchase follow an offer, a product recommendation or a reminder?

These checks turn a headline figure into something you can act on. They also help you spot gaps in your understanding without assuming the platform has observed every action a customer took.

Give sales time to arrive—and check where they land

A campaign sent near month-end creates an easy reporting trap.

Suppose Example Goods sends a message on 30 October and a recipient orders on 2 November. A report organised around send date can place the attributed result in October, while an order-date report places the purchase in November.

Omnisend documents message-date attribution and an order-date option in Sales Reports. Its guidance also states that later refunds or cancellations do not remove the original attributed sales value. Those details matter when reconciling reports. Omnisend's reporting rules

Allow comparable campaigns the same observation time. A message sent yesterday should not be judged against one that has had several weeks to collect orders.

For a monthly management review, decide when results are preliminary and when you will revisit them. Use a consistent reporting cut-off and keep a separate record of commercial adjustments. The objective is a repeatable comparison, not a dashboard that can never change.

Do not add every platform's claim together

Now imagine one £100 order at Example Goods. The shopper discovers a product through an advert, later clicks an email and finally returns directly to buy.

An advertising system and an email system may both claim credit under their own rules. Adding those claims does not turn the £100 order into £200 of store revenue.

Shopify provides multiple attribution models, including first click, last click and linear. Its marketing reports can therefore answer different questions depending on the model selected. Shopify also explains that marketing-attributed sales can differ from other sales reports. Shopify: marketing reports

Use store orders as the common reference when investigating overlap. Match a small sample using order identifiers where available, handle customer information securely and compare the journey each report describes.

Do not force every report to agree. Explain the differences that affect your decision, and investigate unexplained discrepancies before presenting the figures as settled.

Compare email results with the whole store

Suppose Example Goods reports these illustrative figures using consistent definitions:

MetricSeptemberOctober
Store sales£40,000£36,000
Email-attributed sales£8,000£10,000
Email-attributed share20%About 28%

Email's attributed share has increased while store sales have fallen. This does not prove email caused the fall, and it does not prove the email programme delivered business growth.

It gives you questions to investigate. Did paid traffic decline? Was a popular product unavailable? Did more returning customers buy while fewer new customers arrived? Did attribution settings change?

The reverse can happen too: store sales grow faster than email-attributed sales, reducing email's share even while its attributed value rises.

Review sales, orders, traffic, customer mix and the promotional calendar together. A percentage is useful context, not a target that should rise regardless of what happens elsewhere.

Bring costs into the conversation

A campaign can generate orders and still be commercially disappointing.

Imagine a hypothetical £1,000 of sales after discounts, excluding tax. Product costs are £450, fulfilment and payment costs are £120, and the store absorbs £80 of delivery costs. That leaves £350 before marketing costs and overheads.

This simplified calculation is not a profit statement, and it does not establish incremental impact. It shows why sales revenue alone cannot tell you what the campaign left behind.

Account for incentives once: if the sales figure already includes discounts, do not subtract the same discount again. Review returns and cancellations consistently, then include the relevant software, service and campaign production costs when assessing the programme.

Our discounts versus free shipping guide explains how the basket changes an offer's economics. The same discipline belongs in reporting.

Test incremental impact when the audience supports it

For an eligible promotional campaign, consider randomly assigning comparable subscribers to a message group and a holdout group that does not receive that campaign. Keep transactional messages intact and define how other marketing will be handled.

Compare outcomes across everyone assigned to each group, not just people who open or click. Engaged recipients select themselves in ways that make them a poor substitute for a randomly assigned comparison group.

For example, if equally sized groups produce £2.40 and £2.10 of sales per assigned subscriber over the agreed window, the observed difference is £0.30. It is not automatically a reliable or repeatable lift. You still need to assess uncertainty, group size, costs, overlap and the buying cycle.

A small list may not support a confident conclusion. Avoid a universal sample-size rule or declaring victory after a few orders. Also keep the claim narrow: withholding one campaign tests that campaign under those conditions, not the value of the entire email channel.

Finish the monthly review with a decision

A useful reporting meeting should end with one or two specific actions:

  1. Record the reporting definitions and attribution settings.
  2. Compare campaigns and automations separately.
  3. Reconcile representative orders and explain material differences.
  4. Review store outcomes and contribution alongside attributed sales.
  5. Choose a change to test and define the evidence you will use.

Perhaps the next step is improving product guidance in a welcome flow. Perhaps it is testing a less expensive incentive or correcting an inconsistent report. The numbers should help you choose, rather than simply decorate a monthly presentation.
